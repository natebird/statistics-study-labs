Bar Charts and Pie Charts Study Lab

Bar charts and pie charts turn a frequency table into a picture. Bar charts make comparisons between categories easy; pie charts show how one whole is divided. Switch between them, then see how a truncated axis can mislead.

2. Key ideas

Bars

Bar chart

One bar per category, height equal to its count or proportion. Bars are separated by gaps, and the vertical axis starts at 0.

Slices

Pie chart

Each slice's angle is its share of the whole. Use it only when the categories are parts of one whole that adds to 100%.

Scale

Counts vs. proportions

A bar chart can show frequencies or relative frequencies. The shapes are identical; only the axis labels change.

Describe

Compare frequencies

Describe categorical graphs by naming the most and least common categories and how much they differ. Don't use shape words like skewed.

Watch out

Misleading graphs

Truncated axes, pictographs with growing areas, and 3-D effects distort comparisons. Readers compare areas and heights, so those must be proportional to the data.

Order

Ordinal categories

Some categorical variables have a natural order (sizes, letter grades, agree to disagree). Keep that order on the graph.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Label both axes, including what is being counted (“Number of students”), and title the graph.
  • Start the vertical axis of a bar chart at 0 and use equal widths for all bars.
  • When describing the graph, compare categories with numbers from the graph: “Netflix (37%) was chosen about twice as often as Disney+ (15%).”
  • If a question asks why a graph is misleading, name the feature (truncated axis, unequal areas) and explain the distortion.

Common mistakes

  • Making the bars touch, which makes the graph look like a histogram.
  • Describing a bar chart's shape as skewed or symmetric.
  • Using a pie chart when respondents could choose more than one answer.
  • Judging a difference from bar heights without checking where the axis starts.
