2. Key ideas

Vocabulary

Parameter vs. statistic

A parameter describes the population (p, μ, σ) and is usually unknown. A statistic describes a sample (p̂, x̄, s) and is used as a point estimate of the parameter.

Sampling distribution

Every statistic has one

The sampling distribution is the distribution of a statistic's values over all possible samples of the same size. Simulation approximates it by taking many samples.

Bias

Is it right on average?

An estimator is unbiased when the mean of its sampling distribution equals the parameter. p̂, x̄, and s² (with n − 1) are unbiased. Bad sampling methods cause bias.

Variability

How much do estimates disagree?

Variability is the spread of the sampling distribution. Larger random samples give less variability. The population size barely matters if the sample is under 10% of it.

Trade-offs

Bias and variability are different

A biased method with a huge sample gives precise but wrong answers. An unbiased method with a tiny sample is right on average but often far off. You want both low.

Target picture

Darts on a bullseye

Bias is aiming off-center; variability is how scattered the darts are. Low bias + low variability means tight shots centered on the bullseye.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Define bias by the center: "The estimator is unbiased because the mean of its sampling distribution equals the parameter."
  • When a dotplot of simulated estimates is given, compare its center to the parameter (bias) and its spread (variability), and mention both.
  • To choose between two estimators, name the trade-off: "Estimator A is unbiased but more variable; Estimator B has slight bias but much less variability."
  • Say what a larger sample does: it reduces variability. It does not reduce bias from a flawed sampling method.
  • Use context: "p̂ estimates the proportion of all students at the school who want a later start."

Common mistakes

  • Saying an unbiased estimator gives the right answer every time. It is right on average, not in every sample.
  • Thinking a bigger sample removes bias. It only makes the estimates cluster more tightly around whatever center they already have.
  • Mixing up variability of the data (s) with variability of a statistic (the SD of its sampling distribution).
  • Believing a sample must be a large fraction of the population to be accurate. Sample size matters, not the fraction.
  • Using the word "bias" to mean any error. Bias is systematic error in one direction.
