Parameters of Random Variables Study Lab

The mean of a random variable, also called its expected value, is the long-run average of its values over many repetitions. Its standard deviation measures how far the values typically fall from that mean. Edit a distribution, run it thousands of times, and see how changing units (Y = a + bX) affects the mean and standard deviation.

1. Expected value and standard deviation

Each value is weighted by its probability.

μ = E(X) = Σ x · P(x) σ² = Σ (x − μ)² · P(x) σ = √σ²

2. Key ideas

Expected value

The long-run average

E(X) = Σ x·P(x) is what the average of X settles toward over many, many repetitions. It does not have to be a possible value: the expected number of heads in 3 flips is 1.5.

Spread

Standard deviation σX

σX measures how far the values of X typically fall from μX, in the units of X. Find the variance first, then take the square root.

Law of large numbers

Averages settle down

As the number of independent trials grows, the average of the observed values gets closer and closer to μ. In the short run, averages can bounce around a lot.

Transform

Y = a + bX

Adding a shifts the mean by a but leaves the spread alone. Multiplying by b multiplies the mean by b, the standard deviation by |b|, and the variance by b². The shape does not change (a negative b mirrors it).

Context

Interpret both parameters

Say what μX and σX mean for the situation, with units: "over many households, the average is about 1.8 cars, and the number of cars typically varies by about 0.93 car from that mean."

Notation

Parameters, not statistics

μX and σX describe the whole probability model, so they are parameters. The mean and SD of simulated or observed data are statistics that estimate them.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Show the expected value as a sum: "E(X) = 0(0.10) + 1(0.25) + 2(0.40) + 3(0.25) = 1.80."
  • Interpret the mean as a long-run average in context: "If many households were selected, the average number of cars would be about 1.8."
  • Interpret σ as typical variation: "The number of cars typically varies by about 0.93 from the mean of 1.8."
  • For a transformation, show both rules with numbers: "Y = 15 + 10X, so μY = 15 + 10(1.8) = $33 and σY = 10(0.93) = $9.30. The $15 does not change σ."
  • Calculator work (Desmos or TI-84) is fine for the arithmetic, but write the formula with numbers in it.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the square root: σ is √σ², not σ² itself.
  • Using Sx instead of σx from TI-84 1-Var Stats L1, L2 (or stdev in Desmos). A probability model's σ weights by probabilities; it does not divide by n − 1.
  • Letting a constant change the standard deviation. A flat fee shifts the mean only.
  • Using b instead of |b| when b is negative, or multiplying the variance by b instead of b². An SD is never negative.
  • Saying "we expect X to be 1.8" for a count. Say the long-run average is 1.8.
