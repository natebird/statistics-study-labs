Parameters of Random Variables Study Lab

The mean of a random variable, also called its expected value, is the long-run average of its values over many repetitions. Its standard deviation measures how far the values typically fall from that mean. Edit a distribution, run it thousands of times, and see how unit changes and combining variables affect the mean and standard deviation.

Means always add or subtract. If X and Y are independent, their variances add, whether you add or subtract the variables. Standard deviations never add directly.

2. Key ideas

Expected value

The long-run average

E(X) = Σ x·P(x) is what the average of X settles toward over many, many repetitions. It does not have to be a possible value: the expected number of heads in 3 flips is 1.5.

Spread

Standard deviation σX

σX measures how far the values of X typically fall from μX, in the units of X. Find the variance first, then take the square root.

Law of large numbers

Averages settle down

As the number of independent trials grows, the average of the observed values gets closer and closer to μ. In the short run, averages can bounce around a lot.

Transform

Y = a + bX

Adding a shifts the mean by a but leaves the spread alone. Multiplying by b multiplies the mean by b and the standard deviation by |b|. The shape does not change.

Combine

Variances add

For independent X and Y, Var(X + Y) = Var(X − Y) = σX² + σY². Subtracting does not cancel variability; a difference is still uncertain in both directions.

Notation

Parameters, not statistics

μX and σX describe the whole probability model, so they are parameters. The mean and SD of simulated or observed data are statistics that estimate them.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Show the expected value as a sum: "E(X) = 0(0.10) + 1(0.25) + 2(0.40) + 3(0.25) = 1.80."
  • Interpret the mean as a long-run average in context: "If many households were selected, the average number of cars would be about 1.8."
  • Interpret σ as typical variation: "The number of cars typically varies by about 0.93 from the mean of 1.8."
  • When combining, state independence before adding variances: "Since the two times are independent, σ = √(4² + 3²) = 5 minutes."
  • Calculator work is fine for the arithmetic, but write the formula with numbers in it.

Common mistakes

  • Adding standard deviations: √(4² + 3²) = 5, not 4 + 3 = 7.
  • Subtracting variances for X − Y. Variances always add for independent variables.
  • Letting a constant change the standard deviation. A flat fee shifts the mean only.
  • Confusing 3X (one value tripled, SD × 3) with X₁ + X₂ + X₃ (three independent values, SD × √3).
  • Saying "we expect X to be 1.8" for a count. Say the long-run average is 1.8.
