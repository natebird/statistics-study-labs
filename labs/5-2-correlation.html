2. Key ideas

Definition

What r measures

The correlation r measures the direction and strength of the linear association between two quantitative variables. The sign gives the direction; the size gives the strength.

Range

Always from −1 to 1

r = 1 or r = −1 only when every point lies exactly on a line. r near 0 means a weak linear association. Values near ±1 mean the points hug a line closely.

No units

Unit changes do not affect r

r is computed from z-scores, so it has no units. Converting inches to centimeters, or adding a constant to every value, leaves r unchanged. Swapping x and y also leaves r unchanged.

Linear only

Plot first

r only describes linear relationships. A strong curved pattern can have r near 0, and a curve can also have r near 1. Always look at the scatterplot before interpreting r.

Not resistant

Outliers can change r a lot

Because r uses means and standard deviations, a single unusual point can make r much stronger or much weaker than the pattern of the rest of the data.

Causation

Correlation is not causation

A high r does not mean x causes y. A lurking variable may drive both. Only a well-designed randomized experiment supports a cause-and-effect conclusion.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Interpret r with all three pieces: direction, strength, and linear, plus context. "r = −0.86 indicates a strong, negative, linear association between car age and price."
  • Before using r, state that the scatterplot shows a roughly linear pattern. r is not meaningful for curved data.
  • To get r from data, use Desmos corr(x₁, y₁) (or the r shown by y₁ ~ m x₁ + b) or TI-84 LinReg(a+bx) L₁, L₂ with DiagnosticOn. The number alone earns nothing; interpret it.
  • If a question asks whether x causes y, check how the data were collected. Observational data support only association; random assignment supports causation.
  • Name a plausible lurking variable and explain how it could affect both variables.

Common mistakes

  • Saying r = 0 means "no relationship." It means no linear relationship; a curve may still be strong.
  • Interpreting r as a percent, such as "r = 0.8 means 80% of points are on the line."
  • Saying a correlation of −0.9 is weaker than 0.5. The sign is direction; the size is strength.
  • Concluding causation from a strong correlation in observational data.
  • Thinking r changes when units change, or when the variables are swapped.
