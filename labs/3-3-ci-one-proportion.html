2. Key ideas

Structure

Estimate ± margin of error

Every confidence interval in AP Statistics is statistic ± (critical value) × (standard error of the statistic).

Standard error

SE uses p̂

We don't know p, so we estimate σp̂ with SE = √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n).

Critical value

z* from the confidence level

z* cuts off the middle C% of the standard normal: 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.576 (99%). For 95%: Desmos normaldist(0, 1).inversecdf(0.975), TI-84 invNorm(0.975, 0, 1).

Conditions

Random, 10%, Large counts

Random sample; n ≤ 10% of the population when sampling without replacement; at least 10 successes and 10 failures in the sample.

Width

What changes the margin?

Higher confidence → wider. Larger n → narrower (4× the n gives half the width). p̂ near 0.5 → widest.

Confidence level

It describes the method

About C% of intervals from repeated random samples capture p. A single interval either does or doesn't; we never know which.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Use the four-step process: State (parameter and confidence level), Plan (name the procedure, check conditions), Do (show the formula with numbers), Conclude (interpret in context).
  • Define the parameter in words: "p = the true proportion of all students at the school who feel well rested."
  • Check conditions with numbers: "np̂ = 58 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p̂) = 142 ≥ 10; 200 < 10% of 2,400 students."
  • Name the procedure: "one-sample z-interval for p."
  • Check with a calculator: Desmos zproptest(58, 200), then T₁.conf(0.95).lower and .upper; TI-84 1-PropZInt (x = 58, n = 200, C-Level = 0.95). Calculator output alone does not earn credit: still name the procedure, check conditions, show p̂ ± z*·SE with numbers, and interpret in context.
  • Interpret: "We are 95% confident that the interval from 0.227 to 0.353 captures the true proportion of all students at the school who feel well rested."

Common mistakes

  • Saying "there is a 95% probability that p is in this interval."
  • Interpreting the interval as describing sample proportions instead of the population proportion.
  • Using p instead of p̂ in the standard error, or checking Large Counts with a p you don't know.
  • Rounding a required sample size down. Always round up.
  • Thinking a bigger sample fixes a biased (non-random) sampling method.
