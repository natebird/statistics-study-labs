2. Key ideas

Homogeneity

Several groups, one variable

Use a test for homogeneity when you have separate random samples (or treatment groups) and compare the distribution of one categorical variable across them.

Independence

One group, two variables

Use a test for independence when you have a single random sample and record two categorical variables for each individual, asking whether they are associated.

Hypotheses

Written in words

Homogeneity: H₀ says the distributions are the same in every population. Independence: H₀ says there is no association between the two variables.

Expected counts

What H₀ predicts

Expected count = (row total)(column total) ÷ table total. These are the counts you would expect if the rows all had the same distribution.

Conditions

Random, 10%, expected ≥ 5

Data from random samples or random assignment; each sample at most 10% of its population; and every expected count at least 5.

Degrees of freedom

(r − 1)(c − 1)

df depends only on the size of the table. A 3 × 4 table has (3 − 1)(4 − 1) = 6 degrees of freedom.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Name the test exactly: "chi-square test for homogeneity" or "chi-square test for independence." The design decides which.
  • Write hypotheses in context with words, naming the populations or variables.
  • Show at least one expected count calculation: (80)(72) ÷ 240 = 24.
  • State that all expected counts are at least 5, and give the smallest one.
  • Give df = (r − 1)(c − 1) with numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the test from the table's shape. Both tests use a two-way table; the sampling design decides.
  • Checking that observed counts are at least 5 instead of expected counts.
  • Rounding expected counts to whole numbers.
  • Writing hypotheses about a single proportion, like p₁ = p₂, when there are more than two categories.
  • Using df = n − 1 instead of (r − 1)(c − 1).
