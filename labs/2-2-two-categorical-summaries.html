Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Relative Frequencies Study Lab

Every relative frequency from a two-way table is a count divided by a total. The only question is which total. A joint relative frequency divides by the grand total, a marginal one uses a row or column total over the grand total, and a conditional one divides by the total of the group you are looking within.

2. Key ideas

Joint

"Both" questions

A joint relative frequency answers "what proportion of everyone is in this row and this column?" Divide one cell by the grand total. All the joint relative frequencies add up to 1.

Marginal

One variable only

A marginal relative frequency divides a row total or a column total by the grand total. It describes one variable and ignores the other.

Conditional

"Of the…" questions

A conditional relative frequency looks only inside one group. "Of the seniors, what proportion drive?" divides the senior-and-drive cell by the senior total.

Order matters

Two different conditionals

"Proportion of seniors who drive" and "proportion of drivers who are seniors" use the same cell but different denominators, so they usually give different answers.

Association

Compare conditionals

If the conditional relative frequencies are the same in every group, the variables are not associated. If they differ, they are associated. The difference between two conditional proportions is a simple summary statistic for the association.

Notation

Read the bar as "given"

Later in the unit, the conditional relative frequency of A within group B is written P(A | B), read "the probability of A given B."

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Write the fraction before the decimal: "54/90 = 0.60." The fraction shows the grader which total you used.
  • Describe the group in words: "Of the 90 juniors surveyed, 60% have a pet."
  • To show association, compute a conditional proportion for each group and compare them in one sentence with both numbers.
  • Keep at least three decimal places, or use percents with one decimal.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by the grand total when the question says "of the juniors." That gives a joint relative frequency, not a conditional one.
  • Mixing up the direction of a conditional: "juniors who have pets" vs. "pet owners who are juniors."
  • Comparing counts across groups of different sizes. 80 seniors with pets is more than 54 juniors, but a smaller proportion.
  • Adding conditional relative frequencies from different groups. They each add to 1 within their own group.
